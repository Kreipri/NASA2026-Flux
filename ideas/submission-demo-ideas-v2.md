## Three distinct directions
| Direction | Seven-slide approach | 30-second approach | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. One choice changes the mission — recommended** | Follow dirty panels → costs → clean → changed power → lesson | A short actual interaction with before/after | Easiest to understand and verify; less room for characters. |
| B. Your first day as commander | Introduce crew, get one ask, choose a job, see the report card and return home | Curio’s invitation → job → crew/ending | Warm and age-appropriate; risks becoming a character trailer without mechanics. |
| C. From NASA archive to a child’s decision | Source record → simplified model → storm alert → shelter → explanation | Source card → shelter job/cost → result | Best science emphasis; current incomplete numerical mapping needs explicit limits, and free shelter alert must not be presented as a costly action. |

Use A with a brief Curio introduction and one clear NASA-source slide. Use the paid cleaning flow; do not claim the archived storm numerically causes dust or solar-panel loss. NASA dust research explains the physical concept; the dust event and numerical costs are game rules.

## Recommended seven-slide storyboard

### 1 — Can you keep a Moon base running?
**Copy:** “Space Base Commander. A mission-planning game for ages 7–12.”
**Visual:** Actual title/base screen, project name, team and registered member names.
**Purpose:** Identify project, team and Junior Astronaut Mission Trainer challenge immediately.

### 2 — Sunlight keeps your base alive
**Copy:** “Sunlight makes power. Dark craters may hold ice. Exploring means planning what you can afford.”
**Visual:** Base scene with only solar panels, shelter and crater labelled.
**Purpose:** Explain the one lesson: where you work changes power and risk. Mark scene as an illustration; add relevant NASA science source footer.

### 3 — You have one crew—and limited time
**Copy:** “Balance power, air and radiation. Every job uses crew time. Curio helps you understand your options.”
**Visual:** Actual job panel with labelled costs and meters, using one selected job rather than every menu.
**Purpose:** Explain the playable system. Crew energy limits work; avoid implying four equally complex resource economies or claiming every alert choice is already costly.

### 4 — Clean now, or use that time elsewhere?
**Copy:** “Dust lowers solar power. Cleaning takes crew time, air and energy. Choose a job and see what changed.”
**Visual:** Large before → chosen cleaning job → actual result/recap. Use the exact run’s numbers, not invented deltas. Cleaning removes dust; power restoration appears in the appropriate weekly calculation, so do not fake an immediate battery gain.
**Purpose:** Show one authentic trade-off and its consequence.
**Source:** NASA dust research: https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/kennedy/nasa-technology-helps-guard-against-lunar-dust/ .

### 5 — Real NASA records, clearly labelled
**Copy:** “Archived solar-event records: NASA DONKI. Radiation measurements near the Moon: CRaTER/LRO through NASA RadLab. Game costs and exposure points are simplified.”
**Visual:** Readable in-game source card plus a small real archive excerpt/date; label “May 2024 archive—not live.”
**Purpose:** Explain what the data actually contributes. Cite original sources; do not suggest weekly exposure is fully derived from measurements.

### 6 — Try, see why, plan again
**Copy:** “A guided first week. Results after each job. A mission report and a story shaped by your choices.”
**Visual:** Tutorial + week recap + actual ending moment, at most three large crops.
**Purpose:** Show the feedback loop and replay, rather than an unsupported claim of measured learning gains. Small line: “Child playtesting is a next step.”

### 7 — Your crew is waiting
**Copy:** “Play the prototype. What would you do differently next time?”
**Visual:** Public playable URL/QR, repository and full credits link, team names if needed. Keep all text readable.
**Credit footer:** “Layout wireframe and selected vectors: Bea. Workflow, code and other vectors: AI-assisted. NASA archive/science credits: see linked references. Independent educational prototype; no NASA endorsement.”
**Purpose:** Give judges access and honest scope. Next steps can be one line: “Refine evidence tracking, data mapping and child usability.”

## Recommended 30-second cut

| Time | Actual screen/action | Spoken line |
|---|---|---|
| 0–4 s | Base/title; persistent small project/team strip | “Can you keep a Moon base running?” |
| 4–9 s | Power, air and radiation labels; dirty-panel alert | “In Space Base Commander, children balance power, air and radiation.” |
| 9–17 s | Open paid cleaning job, show time/costs, execute it | “Cleaning panels helps restore solar power, but uses crew time and supplies.” |
| 17–22 s | Actual result, then relevant weekly recap; label time cut | “Choose a job. See what changed. Plan again.” |
| 22–27 s | Readable NASA archive/source card | “NASA archives and lunar science ground the mission.” |
| 27–30 s | Ending/base plus URL; hold credits | “Your crew is waiting.” |

Narration is about 60 words; record and measure instead of assuming the timing. End at or below 30 seconds including all title and credits frames. Leave the last frame still long enough to read. Use captions, clear labels and no unnecessary camera motion. No music is required; music adds another source/license to track.

**Small persistent footer when sources appear:** “NASA DONKI • NASA OSDR RadLab / CRaTER-LRO • ARCHIVE”. Put a readable full-credits URL on the end card. Include registered team/member identification without forcing a long spoken roll call. Label sped-up or time-jumped footage. A seven-week mission cannot genuinely play in 30 seconds.

## Other short video hooks
- **B, character-led:** “Meet Curio. Your first job: keep the crew breathing.” Follow running the air system, its power cost, result and lesson. More personable; less direct sunlight teaching.
- **C, archive-led:** “This solar storm really happened. Your Moon mission is a simulation.” Show the dated source card, a paid shelter job and resulting gameplay. Strong science hook; clearly separate historical observations from simulated exposure and avoid the free shelter alert shortcut.

## Claims to avoid
- “Live NASA radiation”, “NASA-certified”, “scientifically accurate simulator”, “proven to improve learning”, “every choice costs resources” while free alert shortcuts remain, or “no AI-generated graphics” when AI authored vector graphics.
- A large percent improvement or radiation reduction without an actual verified run/source.
- Showing unimplemented Mars play or runtime AI conversation as an existing feature.
