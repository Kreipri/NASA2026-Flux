# Character concepts v2: rover legacy, animal tributes, real astronaut backgrounds

**2026-11-15 — earlier concept discussion.** `character-concepts-v3.md` is current: Bea clarified that Curio is the helper AI, separate from the exploration rover. The physical rover/recharge/sampling concept below is no longer proposed for Curio. Research references and the profession/history distinctions remain useful; names/species are unapproved drafts.

## 1. A personified rover with a real story

Bea prefers a spinoff of a familiar rover, such as Curiosity, whose real history gives the character meaning. This replaces the earlier agent recommendation to lead with CADRE.

| Inspiration | Story and personality potential | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Curiosity — recommended for character/story** | Its name was chosen by 12-year-old Clara Ma. Make our companion an eager question-asker: notices odd details, pauses for interesting rocks, invites the child to investigate | Real Curiosity is a Mars rover using radioisotope power. A miniature solar-powered Moon companion must be identified as an original fictional adaptation |
| **Opportunity — strongest fit for the sunlight mechanic** | A solar-powered rover designed for a 90-day mission that explored Mars for almost 15 years. Inspires a patient, resourceful companion that plans before pushing onward | The real mission ended after loss of contact during a dust storm. Keep endurance distinct from teaching children to ignore fatigue or danger |
| **Sojourner — strongest small-companion scale** | NASA's first Mars rover was only about microwave size and used solar power. Inspires a small, modest trailblazer: little rover, useful discoveries | Less aligned with Bea's preference for a widely recognized Curiosity-like character; a camera mast added for expression would be our adaptation |

**Recommended concept:** an original training rover inspired by Curiosity, working nickname **Curio** (not locked). Six wheels, a camera mast that turns/tilts like a head, compact instrument body, small sampling arm. Friendly expression comes from real-looking cameras and motion; no need for a human mouth. New artwork and proportions.

**Fictional character hook:** Curio's mission is to help a new commander learn to explore. It treats questions as progress and sometimes gets absorbed in an interesting rock. It knows instruments; the player decides priorities. Example: "That rock can wait. Do we have enough power to get home?"

**Real backstory hook:** a child named the real Curiosity rover. That gives our audience a connection without inventing a NASA biography for Curio. Optional source-card text: "Curio is our made-up rover, inspired by NASA's Curiosity. A 12-year-old named the real rover."

For the existing engine, Curio can recharge from the solar-powered base. If it instead gets its own solar panels, label that as our fictional design. Do not give real Curiosity solar panels, claim it visited the Moon, or invent a real NASA successor project. Personification is story; engineering facts have source cards. Existing rover work still costs power/time and does not provide free science or a new resource meter.

## 2. Animals in space as Easter eggs

**Judgment:** worthwhile as a quiet, respectful layer. The animal crew should be original fictional astronauts, with their own lives and personalities. Do not present research animals as willing volunteers or make a real animal's suffering into a cute character backstory.

Three treatments:
1. **Subtle patches/accessories — recommended.** A portrait detail or keepsake links to an optional, sourced profile note. The history stays discoverable without becoming the mission plot. Trade-off: many players will miss it, which is acceptable for an Easter egg.
2. **Names/species as direct homages.** A dog referencing Belka or Strelka, or a squirrel monkey referencing Baker. Stronger recognition; greater risk of confusing the fictional astronaut with the historical animal or making a child's search unexpectedly upsetting.
3. **A small optional history page.** Freer species choices for the main crew, with tributes collected separately. Clearest factual context; extra content/UI scope and a weaker connection to the characters.

Verified anchors:
- **Baker:** squirrel monkey, recovered after the 1959 suborbital flight; lived until 1984, age 27. The flight was an Army mission, not something to relabel as a NASA flight. NASA's history page documents it.
- **Belka and Strelka:** dogs on a Soviet orbital flight in 1960 who returned alive. Credit the Soviet program, not NASA, even when NASA hosts the source. Do not repeat the NASA StarChild page's incorrect broad "first living creatures to return" claim; suborbital survivors preceded them.
- **Laika:** died during her Soviet orbital flight. A direct cheerful Laika teammate would carry a much heavier association; not the recommended first homage for this game.

Suggested direction, still unapproved: explore a **dog and a squirrel monkey** as two of the crewmates; leave the third open instead of forcing a historical match. A profile note could say, "This character's patch honors Baker, a real squirrel monkey who returned from a spaceflight in 1959." This identifies exactly what is being honored. Survival does not imply the experiments were harmless. If the history is explained, keep it truthful and age-appropriate; do not invent a happy ending for animals who died.

The previous beaver/rabbit/mole cast is no longer the lead recommendation. Choose roles and personalities independently of species: the monkey need not be comic relief, and the dog need not be obedient or the leader.

## 3. Astronaut professions: preserve the earlier fact check

**Bea is right: this was already checked** in `storyline-v1.md`, section 0, and F23 in `fact-sources.md`. V1 described backgrounds, but its "engineer / doctor / geologist" framing could still imply three required crew slots. The M4 briefing draft actually reverted to those labels; corrected in this pass.

Real distinctions:
- **Profession/background:** engineering, medicine, geology, etc. NASA recruits across these fields; they are credible backgrounds, not a required combination on every mission.
- **Flight assignment:** commander, pilot, mission specialist, flight engineer, etc., depending on spacecraft/program. These do not map one-to-one to degrees. Geologist Harrison Schmitt's Apollo 17 assignment was lunar module pilot.
- **Onboard responsibility:** maintaining systems, collecting samples, or coordinating crew health. Astronauts share broad training and receive mission-specific training. A medically trained crew member need not be a physician; NASA also has flight surgeons supporting crews from Mission Control. A real physician-astronaut is nevertheless plausible, e.g. Anil Menon.

Recommended player-facing profiles:

| Common identity | Background line | Simple duty label in our fictional mission |
|---|---|---|
| Astronaut | "Trained as an engineer" | **Base systems** |
| Astronaut | "Trained as a doctor" | **Crew health** |
| Astronaut | "Trained as a geologist — a rock scientist" | **Exploring** |

All can help with repairs, health procedures, and science according to training. The labels describe who takes the lead in this fictional mission; do not lock jobs by character or state that NASA always sends this trio. Do not make one person the only astronaut who understands storm safety. No extra pilot character is needed for an outpost-management game that does not simulate flying. Exact flight assignments can stay out of the UI when they add no useful decision.

Suggested briefing: "Your crew are all astronauts. They trained in engineering, medicine, and rocks. Everyone helps with different jobs. Their plans use the same power and time. You make the call, Commander."

The useful simplification is giving each character an understandable area of focus; accuracy does not require replacing those plausible backgrounds.
