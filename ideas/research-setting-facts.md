## Big update: the Artemis plan changed in 2026 (verify before quoting)
- Artemis III (2027) is now a low Earth orbit rendezvous/docking test with commercial landers, **no landing**. Artemis IV (2028) is the first crewed landing. [nasa.gov/humans-in-space/artemis/, dated 2026-10-21]
- NASA's 2026-03-24 "Ignition" event announced the **Moon Base** and said it would "pause Gateway in its current form." [nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-unveils-initiatives-to-achieve-americas-national-space-policy/]
- Artemis II flew a crewed lunar test mission starting 2026-04-01, about 10 days. [nasa.gov/blogs/workforce-updates/2026/05/27/...]
- **Impact:** our old assumption of a "6.5-day Artemis III surface stay for 2" is outdated. Don't use it.

## Setting: the Moon Base (south pole)
- Located "near the lunar South Pole." Phase 1 (to 2029) robotic. Phase 2 (2029-2032) early power, cargo, logistics, comms. **Phase 3 (2032+) a permanent outpost where astronauts live and work for extended periods.** No habitat design details given. [nasa.gov/reference/moonbase-about/]
- Site choice weighs lighting, terrain, and comms access. Region spans "hundreds of square miles." [nasa.gov/moon-base-frequently-asked-questions/, updated 2026-11-07]
- Moon Base I: Blue Origin Blue Moon Mk1 lander to the **Shackleton Connecting Ridge**, no earlier than fall 2026 (not confirmed whether it flew). [nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-provides-update-on-moon-base-rovers-landers-missions/]
- Rovers: Lunar Terrain Vehicles (Astrolab CLV-1, Lunar Outpost Pegasus) targeted 2028; nuclear-powered PROMISE rover; JAXA pressurized rover expected; VIPER to map water ice; JPL MoonFall hop-drones target 2028. [same + moonbase-about]
- Older 2020 Base Camp concept: fixed habitat for up to 4 astronauts for a month, unpressurized LTV (range over 12 miles), pressurized Habitable Mobility Platform, energy storage for up to 8 days. [nasa.gov/blogs/artemis/2020/10/28/lunar-living-nasas-artemis-base-camp-concept/]

## Light and temperature
- **Shackleton rim:** three rim points are "collectively sunlit for more than 90% of the year." The Moon's axial tilt is ~1.5 degrees, so the Sun hugs the horizon. The crater floor is "perpetually in shadow." [science.nasa.gov/resource/shackleton-craters-illuminated-rim-shadowed-interior/]
- **Permanently shadowed regions (PSRs):** ~3% of the south pole area. Some have had no sunlight for up to 2 billion years. Temperatures as low as **-414 F**. [svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/11230]
- Another NASA page: PSRs never rise above **-280 F** (snippet-only). [nasa.gov/podcasts/curious-universe/why-the-moons-icy-south-pole-is-a-hot-target-for-nasa/]
- Administrator remarks (May 2026): sunlit surface over 250 degrees, darkness below -200, PSRs below -400 (units not stated, presumably F).
- Lunar night is ~14.5 Earth days **away from the poles**. [nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/glenn/nasas-fission-surface-power-project-energizes-lunar-exploration]
- Older illumination studies (snippet-only): best Shackleton-rim site ~0.71-0.73 average illumination in the worst case; energy storage for **71-113 hours**; one site lit 94.4% of the year with a **longest eclipse of 101 hours**. [ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20070034951]
- Sources disagree: rim sunlight is quoted between ~70% and over 90% of the year, depending on the site and study.

## Water ice
- LCROSS (2009) hit Cabeus: about 5% water by weight, with early estimates ~1% (snippet-only). Another figure: 5.6 +/- 2.9%, with a 2020 reanalysis of 6.3 +/- 1.6% (snippet-only). [science.nasa.gov/mission/lcross/, ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20110012430]
- LRO's LEND detector found likely ice in Cabeus, Shoemaker, and Faustini. NASA says the measurements are "not super precise." (snippet-only)
- Uses: drinking water, or split into hydrogen and oxygen for rocket fuel. VIPER will map the ice.
- **Uncertain:** ice abundance (1-6%), and NASA's own neutron data disagree in places. It may be patchy or buried. The game should not promise "an ice lake."

## Power
- **Fission Surface Power:** 40 kW electrical, under 6 metric tons, about 33 US homes, 10-year goal unattended. Can run through the 14.5-day night and sit in PSRs. Target: early 2030s. [nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/glenn/nasas-fission-surface-power-project-energizes-lunar-exploration]
- NASA is also developing radioisotope heating for cold nights and shadowed regions. [moonbase-about]
- The 2020 concept said 10 kW; the later requirement is 40 kW. The 2026 release gave no kW figure for the lunar reactor.
- Solar array and battery specs: **not found.**

## Radiation
- The Moon has "no protection from radiation and solar particle events." (Administrator remarks, May 2026)
- Chang'e-4 (China/Germany, **not NASA**) measured **1,369 uSv/day** on the far side, vs ISS galactic cosmic rays 523 uSv/day (731 with the South Atlantic Anomaly). Roughly 200x Earth's surface dose. Measured at solar minimum, no solar storm (snippet-only). [sci.news secondary source]
- Orion keeps the dose from a large solar particle event at or below 150 mSv (snippet-only).
- Shelter: crews build a shelter from stowage bags on Orion. On the Moon, regolith can be piled over shelters. A very rare "Centennial" event needs extra ~10 cm water-equivalent shielding (snippet-only). [science.nasa.gov/science-research/heliophysics/how-nasa-will-protect-astronauts-from-space-radiation-at-the-moon/]
- Long-term: NASA estimate of under 100 to about 350 mSv over 12 months (snippet-only).
- LRO CRaTER numbers on a NASA page: not found (but we have the RadLab data itself).

## Communications and crew
- Round trip ~2.5 s, so ~1.25 s each way (derived, snippet-only).
- Many south pole regions don't see Earth for long periods. LunaNet relays are meant to give continuous comms (snippet-only). **Gateway is paused**, so relay assumptions are uncertain.
- Crew size and stay length for the Moon Base: **not stated** in 2026 pages. The FAQ says stays will "grow progressively longer."

## Hazards
- **Dust:** Apollo crews reported sneezing and congestion ("lunar hay fever"). Dust wore down suit boots and seals, clogged mechanisms, and can cause overheating. Particles are sharp, "like tiny pieces of glass," and statically charged so they cling. Full health effects unknown. [science.nasa.gov/biological-physical/what-hazards-are-caused-by-lunar-regolith/]
- Systems must "protect crews from radiation, extreme temperatures, lunar dust." [moon-base FAQ]

## What this means for the game (agent-neutral summary)
- **Safe to state as fact:** pole ridges get sunlight most of the year (>90% for the best rim points), PSR floors are extremely cold and dark, ice exists but amounts are uncertain, the Moon gives no radiation protection, dust is a real hazard, ISS recycles over 98% of water.
- **Fictional (must be labeled):** the crew, their names, the specific habitat layout, the exact mission dates, the story.
- **Careful:** 14-day nights are NOT the pole story. Long dark periods at the ridge are hours (~70-110 h worst case in older studies), not weeks. **Updated by the verification pass:** worst-case storage 73-117 h (verified); longest ground-level shadow at the ridge up to ~11 days in some years. Say "usually hours, worst case a few days".

# Addendum: Is radiation a real threat? What do astronauts use? (2026-11-14)

**Caveat:** only the SRAG "How" page was fetched and read. Everything else is **snippet-only** (from search summaries) and should be verified. Most PDF fetches failed.

## Radiation is a real, top-ranked risk (not just sci-fi)
- Two kinds beyond Earth's magnetic field: steady **galactic cosmic rays (GCR)** and occasional **solar particle events (SPE)**. [ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20140005866] (snippet-only)
- **Shielding differs:** solar protons are "relatively easy to shield" (confirmed on NASA page). GCR is very high energy and hard to block. [nasa.gov/solar-system/nasa-protects-its-superheroes-from-space-weather/]
- **Why the Moon is exposed:** no global magnetic field, almost no atmosphere, so the surface is hit directly. [science.nasa.gov/moon/sun-moonlight/solar-wind] (snippet-only)
- **Risks NASA names:** cancer, central nervous system effects, degenerative tissue effects (e.g. cardiovascular disease), and acute radiation syndrome only from an unshielded exposure to a very large SPE. [ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20140005866] (snippet-only)
- **Limit:** NASA-STD-3001 Rev F (Dec 2023) sets career spaceflight dose below **600 mSv** for all astronauts. Adoption year (2022 vs 2023) not confirmed. SPE design target under a short-term limit of **250 mSv**. [nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/radiation-protection-technical-brief-ochmo.pdf, ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250006168/...] (snippet-only)
- **Typical doses:** ISS about 80-160 mSv per 6 months, Earth background about 2 mSv per year. Moon surface (China's Chang'e-4, **not NASA**): 1,369 uSv/day, about 1.9x the ISS total in the same period. No NASA surface dose figure found. (snippet-only)
- **How NASA reduces risk:** shelters and heavily shielded areas, regolith shielding (estimates range from about 2 m to 3-7 m thick), monitoring and forecasting, limiting time and dose (ALARA = "as low as reasonably achievable"). [srag.jsc.nasa.gov/SpaceRadiation/How/How.cfm] (fetched), others snippet-only.

**Kid-safe wording (agent-drafted, still ours to review):**
1. "On the Moon there is no air or magnetic field to block space radiation, so astronauts get more than on Earth. They protect themselves with shelters, shielding, and by limiting how long they stay."
2. "A big solar storm is a short, serious danger. Astronauts get a warning and hide in the most shielded part of their home until it passes."
- Do NOT say radiation "instantly kills" or that astronauts are in constant danger. NASA's job is keeping dose within limits.

## What astronauts actually use (mostly verified on the SRAG page)
- **Crew Active Dosimeters** (since 2020): each astronaut's dose is logged continuously, sent to ISS laptops, which show total mission dose and recent dose rate. [srag.jsc.nasa.gov/SpaceRadiation/How/How.cfm] (fetched)
- **Onboard alarm:** the ISS RAD instrument "acts as an alarm for crew" when dose rate gets high, mainly useful if contact with the ground is lost. [same] (fetched)
- **Alert chain:** NOAA SWPC forecasters -> SRAG (Space Radiation Analysis Group at NASA Johnson) -> SRAG Space Environment Officers in Mission Control recommend actions to the Flight Surgeon and Flight Control Team -> crew. The last step (controllers telling the crew) is inferred, not stated. SRAG gives 24/7 support for Artemis. [same] (fetched)
- **Recommended actions:** avoid lightly shielded areas, shelter in heavily shielded ones, build a shelter, or delay/end a spacewalk (EVA). [same] (fetched)
- **M2M-SWAO** (Goddard): real-time analysis, 24/7 for Artemis, works with SRAG, NOAA SWPC, CCMC. [science.gsfc.nasa.gov/m2m-swao] (snippet-only)
- **Orion HERA** radiation sensors alert the crew during events (trade press only). Artemis II crew would pile stowage bags as a shelter. (snippet-only, conflicting details)
- **Displays:** ISS uses laptops, and NASA moved emergency procedures to iPads that Mission Control can update (snippet-only). Apollo astronauts wore paper "cuff checklists" on the wrist. [nasa.gov/history/alsj/surcl.html] (snippet-only)
- **Not found:** any official page on alerts or displays for a Moon base, ISS crew-alert wording, or Artemis suit displays.

## Game implications
- Radiation is real, so keep it as a core meter, but call it a **dose budget** (staying under limits), not a death meter.
- Real defenses to offer as choices: shelter in the most shielded module, pile regolith, limit time outside (delay/end a spacewalk), reduce outside work.
- **Comms-loss twist (real):** if the link to Earth drops during a storm, an onboard radiation alarm is the backup.
- Do not state exact Moon surface dose as NASA's measurement. The only figure found is from China's Chang'e-4.
